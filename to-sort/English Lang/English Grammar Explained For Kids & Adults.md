### English Grammar Childs Explanation

Here's a simpler way to understand the grammar and language ideas that English teachers talk about:

**Nouns:** The names of things. A noun can be a person (like "teacher"), a place (like "park"), a thing (like "ball"), or a feeling (like "joy").

**Verbs:** Action words! Verbs tell you what someone or something is doing. Examples: "run," "jump," "eat," or "sleep."

**Adjectives:** Words that describe nouns and tell you what they're like. If you say "the big dog" or "the blue sky," the words "big" and "blue" are adjectives.

**Adverbs:** Words that describe how something happens. "She ran *quickly*" — the word "quickly" tells you *how* she ran. Adverbs often end in "-ly."

**Pronouns:** Short words that replace nouns so you don't have to repeat them. Instead of saying "Sarah is happy and Sarah likes pizza," you say "Sarah is happy and *she* likes pizza." Words like "he," "she," "it," and "they" are pronouns.

**Prepositions:** Little words that show where something is or when it happens. Examples: "in," "on," "under," "beside," "before," and "after."

**Articles:** The tiny words "a," "an," and "the" that come before nouns. Use "a" or "an" for something general ("a dog") and "the" for something specific ("the dog I saw yesterday").

**Sentences:** Groups of words that tell a complete idea. A sentence usually has a subject (who or what) and a verb (what they're doing). Example: "*The cat* jumped."

**Tenses:** Different ways verbs change to show *when* something happens. Past (it already happened): "I walked." Present (it's happening now): "I walk." Future (it will happen): "I will walk."

**Irregular Verbs:** Sneaky verbs that don't follow the normal rules! Instead of "go" → "goed," it's "go" → "went." Same with "eat" → "ate" and "be" → "was."

**Questions:** Sentences that ask for information. They usually start with question words like "who," "what," "where," "why," or "how?" Example: "Where are you going?"

**Punctuation:** The marks you use to help people understand your writing: periods (.), commas (,), question marks (?), and more.

**Modal Verbs:** Special helper verbs that show ability, permission, or possibility. Examples: "can," "could," "must," "should," and "may." Example: "*Can* you help me?"

**Passive Voice:** When the thing being acted on becomes the subject instead of the person doing the action. Example: Instead of "I ate the pizza," you say "The pizza *was eaten* by me."

**Phrasal Verbs:** Verbs that work with other small words to make new meanings. "Give up" means "stop trying," and "look after" means "take care of."

**Gerunds and Infinitives:** Special verb forms that act like nouns. A gerund ends in "-ing" (like "*swimming*"), and an infinitive uses "to" plus a verb (like "to swim").

**Conditional Sentences:** "If-then" sentences about situations that might happen. Example: "*If* it rains tomorrow, *then* we'll play inside."

**Reported Speech:** Telling someone what another person said without using their exact words. Instead of saying "Mom said, 'Eat your vegetables,'" you say "Mom said *that* I should eat my vegetables."

**Quantifiers:** Words that tell you how much or how many of something. Examples: "some," "many," "a few," and "all."

**Comparisons:** Looking at the differences between similar things to understand when to use one versus another — like knowing when to use past simple ("I walked") versus past continuous ("I was walking").

### English Grammar Adults Explanation

 * Adjective: A word that describes or modifies a noun, indicating its quality,
   quantity, or extent (e.g., "big," "blue," "three").
 * Adverb: A word that modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb, often
   describing manner, place, time, or degree (e.g., "quickly," "there," "very").
 * Articles: Words ("a," "an," "the") that specify whether a noun is definite
   (specific) or indefinite (general).
 * Conditional Sentences: Sentences expressing hypothetical situations and their
   consequences, typically using "if" (e.g., "If it rains, we’ll stay home").
 * Gerund and Infinitive: Verb forms used as nouns; gerunds end in "-ing" (e.g.,
   "swimming"), infinitives are "to" + verb (e.g., "to swim").
 * Irregular Verbs: Verbs that don’t follow standard conjugation patterns for
   past tense or past participle (e.g., "go" → "went," "gone").
 * Modals and Modal Auxiliaries: Verbs like "can," "must," "should" that
   indicate ability, permission, obligation, or possibility.
 * Nouns: Words naming people, places, things, or ideas (e.g., "dog," "city,"
   "happiness").
 * Participles: Verb forms used as adjectives or to form certain tenses; present
   participles end in "-ing" (e.g., "running"), past participles often end in
   "-ed" (e.g., "finished").
 * Passive Voice: Sentence structure where the subject receives the action
   (e.g., "The book was read" vs. active "She read the book").
 * Phrasal Verbs: Verbs combined with prepositions or adverbs, creating new
   meanings (e.g., "give up," "look after").
 * Prepositions: Words showing relationships of time, place, or direction (e.g.,
   "in," "on," "under").
 * Pronouns: Words replacing nouns to avoid repetition (e.g., "he," "she," "it,"
   "they").
 * Punctuation: Marks like commas, periods, and quotation marks used to organise
   and clarify written language.
 * Quantifiers: Words indicating quantity or extent, used with nouns (e.g.,
   "some," "many," "few").
 * Questions: Sentences designed to elicit information, often using inversion or
   question words like "who," "what," "why."
 * Reported Speech: Conveying what someone said indirectly (e.g., "She said she
   was tired" vs. direct "I’m tired").
 * Sentences: Groups of words forming a complete thought, typically with a
   subject and predicate.
 * Verbs: Words describing actions, states, or occurrences (e.g., "run," "is,"
   "happen").
 * Tenses: Verb forms indicating when an action occurs, such as past, present,
   or future (e.g., "walked," "walk," "will walk").
 * Comparison of Tenses: Analysing differences between verb tenses to understand
   their specific uses and nuances (e.g., present simple vs. present
   continuous).
